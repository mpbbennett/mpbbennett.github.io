Cosmology

While scholars are greatly divided on cosmological questions, practical magicians know that magic exists which enables communication and travel between places with no connection in conventional geography — known as “planes.” However, such magic is highly difficult and costly, available only to a small number of individuals. Interplanar travel on a scale necessary for invasion or commerce is unknown.

The canonical Moonrise scheme divides the planes into three nested rings. The inner planes — the Prime Material and the four Elementals — share recognisable physics. The adjacent planes — Feywild, Shadowfell, and Border Ethereal — press against the material world and bleed into it at soft places. The outer planes lie further out, reached only by unusual magic, and include the Far Realm and whatever else dwells in the chaotic distances beyond. Not all scholars accept this scheme; a minority tradition in the Arcane Order rejects the nested-ring model in favour of a branching diagram.

The Prime Material Plane

The world of Moonrise itself — an island of regular physics where stone falls and tomorrow follows today. See Regions of Moonrise. Planar theorists sometimes call this “the core” or “the slab”; off-world visitors, when they appear, tend to describe it as unusually stable.

The Border Ethereal

A liminal space adjacent to the material world, used by some forms of magic for concealment and travel. Those who have walked it describe it as a grey fog through which the living world appears as coloured lights and muffled sound. Wards, protective charms, and holy sites show brightly on the ethereal side; iron and cold water darken it.

The etherwalk rite, documented in the third book of the Arcane Order’s Schools, permits a practitioner to spend no more than an hour in the Border Ethereal and covers roughly a mile of material travel in that time. Longer transits are possible but reckless: the deeper ethereal shades into regions no returning scholar has mapped.

The Feywild

The Feywild (or Faerie) is a parallel world which occasionally makes contact with the material plane. It is a strange, dreamlike place in which metaphors become real. Some scholars hypothesise it is not truly another plane at all, but rather a collective emanation of the dreams of intelligent creatures.

Descriptions are difficult because the place accommodates the describer. A stone that was a loaf of bread to a starving visitor becomes a stone again to the next. The landscape is structured by feeling: grief deepens rivers and boredom lengthens roads. Time passes unreliably. A day in the Feywild may be an hour or a year in Moonrise, and the ratio is not fixed.

The Elves and gnomes have a natural connection to the Feywild and are sometimes thought to have originated there. Many other creatures share this connection. The inhabitants of the Feywild are neither good nor evil, but from a material-world perspective they often seem strange and duplicitous.

The Feywild is ruled by a feudal hierarchy. Archfey hold courts comparable to the kingdoms of the material world, though held together more by custom and story than by administration. Two broad courts — most scholars name them the Bright and the Dim — structure the upper levels, but the boundary is porous and a given archfey may shift allegiance across a single conversation. Warlocks sometimes derive their power from pacts with archfey. Such pacts represent one of the few ways archfey attempt to influence the material world, and they are fraught with danger: terms are literal, favours compound, and the contract is enforceable in ways material courts cannot contest.

Contact between Moonrise and the Feywild is most reliable at certain places — old stone circles, forest crossings where paths vanish, lakes with no fish. The Forest Kingdoms sit atop many of these and maintain protocols for passage; elsewhere they are unmarked and dangerous.

The Shadowfell

Scholars disagree on whether the Shadowfell is a plane of its own or merely a name for the more sinister regions of the Feywild. In either case, it is a place of darkness and fear from which few have returned and of which little is known.

What observers have reported is consistent on certain points: a muted sun, landscapes that echo material geography in ruined form, a silence that absorbs speech, and a weight that sits on travellers for months after return. Necromantic magic draws on the Shadowfell, and mediums who contact the dead do so through it. Breach points in Moonrise are rare but known — battlefield sites and the lower levels of certain old dwarven halls.

The Elemental Planes

The elemental planes are literally made from their respective elements — nothing but water, stone, air, and fire. Only very powerful magic can enable survival in such environments.

  • The Plane of Fire is an endless burning, where rivers of flame run between cliffs of molten glass. Volcanic regions of the material world — especially around Vermillion and Azure and certain Dwarven deeps — are the most reliable correspondence points.
  • The Plane of Water is an ocean without surface or floor, lit from within by drifting shoals of luminous fish. Breathable sea zones, documented mostly by Leaguer navigators, permit limited material-side contact.
  • The Plane of Air is emptier than the others: drifting islands of stone, called earthmotes, carried on winds that never still. Earthmote zones above The Cloud-Capital and the Lakelands are thought to have originated as small breaches.
  • The Plane of Earth is the densest and least travelled — endless stone shot through with seams of unmined ore, veined with caverns that no living thing has walked end to end.

Intelligent elementals reside within each, not so unlike humans in many ways, though communication is extremely difficult — their timescales and motivations do not map neatly onto those of material creatures. Above the common elementals, four named elemental lords are recognised by the cults that worship them. The Genasi ritual draws on these lords’ energies to infuse a child in the womb; the cults that perform the rite regard the resulting genasi as a living tie to the plane.

Zones of elemental influence exist in the material world as well — earthmote zones for air, volcanic areas for fire, breathable sea zones for water, deep-stone fissures for earth. Most are uninhabitable to ordinary life but valuable to sorcerers, smiths, and the cults themselves.

The Far Realm and the Outer Planes

Beyond the inner planes lie the outer planes, of which the Far Realm is the most-discussed and worst-understood. Even less is known about these than about other planes. Their defining feature, according to those who claim contact, is weirdness and incomprehensibility. The Far Realm is horrifying rather than evil, and many who have attempted contact have been driven insane.

Some claim the Far Realm is the source of all magic. Others claim it is possible to travel through it to reach other worlds. The aberrations that plague the wood elf forests are believed to originate from the Far Realm or connected underdark regions. Great Old One warlock pacts represent entities from the Far Realms.

A minority tradition within the Arcane Order holds that beyond the chaotic sea of the outer planes lie further islands of ordered reality — “parallel worlds” not unlike Moonrise but different in their geography and in the laws governing magic. On this view, the material plane is not unique but one of an unknown number of islands. Some such worlds are held to be more saturated in magic than Moonrise, with correspondingly greater civilisations; others less. Interplanar empires are posited — powerful entities in high-magic regions using their advantage to establish dominion across the nearer planes — though no Moonrise scholar has produced evidence of one. Most planar theorists regard the parallel-worlds thesis as unproven speculation.

Signs and portents

Something is happening to the planar boundaries. The question is whether this is a local swing or a structural change; scholars disagree on which, and on how urgent it is.

Reports over the last three or four years are consistent enough to be catalogued. Interplanar travellers, always a rumour, are now reliably attested — Wayfinder field offices list twenty-seven credible cases since 100AC, against two in the preceding decade. Magic in general appears stronger and easier: apprentices pass exams that used to kill them, and wild surges are more common. Strange coincidences accumulate — merchants who need a boat meet a stranded captain, lost items are found by the person who most needs them — in patterns some scholars now describe as “dream-logic” intruding on waking causality. A small but growing number of people report intense déjà vu, sometimes sharpening into what feels like memory of events not yet lived.

The Arcane Order has opened a standing committee on the question. The Wayfinders have begun issuing warnings for known breach-prone regions, including the Old Dominion ruinlands, the Sylvan depths, and the Graveport shore. The Congregation treats the trend theologically: an approaching Mending, or an approaching unmaking, depending on the preacher.

Theological Implications

A defining feature of Moonrise’s cosmology is the absence of confirmed Celestials and Devils. Religion operates more like the real world — the existence of the gods is not proven, and clerical magic works even for atheists. Contact with other planes is rare enough that the gods remain a matter of faith, not fact. This is what makes The Three so extraordinary — they are the closest thing to proven divinity that Moonrise has ever seen.
